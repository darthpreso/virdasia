What 'Small-Batch' Actually Means in Clothing

15 August 2026

"Small-batch" appears on everything from genuinely limited runs to mass-produced goods with a story attached. The term is not regulated. What is worth understanding is the actual mechanics, because they explain why small-batch clothing costs what it does and why it disappears.

It starts with minimum order quantities

Every factory has an MOQ — the smallest run it will accept. For most established manufacturers that is 300 to 1,000 units per colourway, per size. That number exists because setup dominates the cost: patterning, grading, sampling, threading machines, calibrating embroidery. Those costs are the same whether you make 50 pieces or 5,000.

Small-batch means finding manufacturers who will run 30 to 100 units, and accepting the cost structure that follows. Per-unit cost rises sharply as volume falls, because the setup is amortised over far fewer garments.

What that changes about the clothes

Unit economics. A jacket produced at 50 units can cost several times per unit what the same jacket costs at 5,000. That is the honest reason small-batch pricing sits where it does — not a markup for scarcity.

What gets made at all. At volume, a design must appeal broadly enough to sell thousands. At 50 units it only has to find 50 people. That is why small runs can be specific in ways mass production cannot justify — tonal embroidery, an unusual colourway, a reference most buyers will not catch.

Restocking. Re-running a small batch means paying the setup cost again, and fabric lots differ between runs — dye batches vary, and a second run rarely matches the first exactly. This is why small-batch pieces usually do not return rather than brands manufacturing hype.

Why waitlists exist

With a 50-unit run, guessing the size split wrong is expensive and unfixable. Make too many larges and they sit; too few mediums and you disappoint the people who wanted one.

A waitlist is the cheapest way to find out before committing to production. It is demand information ahead of a decision that cannot be undone — which is also why waitlist spots are capped per piece. The cap reflects the run, not artificial scarcity.

How to tell the difference

Genuine small-batch operations tend to publish specifics: fabric weights in GSM, fibre composition, actual garment measurements, run sizes. Vague copy — "premium materials", "carefully crafted" — usually means there is nothing specific to say.

Check whether pieces come back. A brand that restocks a bestseller indefinitely is running volume production, whatever the copy says. That is not dishonest — it is just a different thing.
